Vrinda Sethi — Founder & Creative Director

“I learned to sew
before I learned
to draw.”

Vrinda grew up in the back room of her grandmother's tailoring shop off Kinari Bazaar, Chandni Chowk, watching gota-patti go onto other people's wedding clothes. She read fashion design at NIFT Delhi, spent three years cutting for a Mumbai couture house, and came back to Delhi in 2009 with four karigars, one pit loom and a workroom above a car-parts shop.

The house now dresses roughly six hundred brides and grooms a year and still refuses to license its name. Vrinda approves every embroidery sample personally — a bottleneck she has been advised to remove eleven times and has not.

The Crafts

Five clusters, paid by the piece

Zardozi & Dabka

Delhi — in house

Metal-wire embroidery worked on a wooden adda frame with a hooked ari needle. Twelve karigars sit to a frame on the largest bridal pieces. The house keeps this craft entirely in-house because the tension of the wire cannot be inspected remotely.

Chikankari

Lucknow — 34 karigars

Shadow work in untwisted cotton thread — murri, phanda, jaali and bakhiya stitches. The jaali is not cut; threads are teased apart with a needle so the fabric is never weakened. One kurta takes six weeks.

Bandhani

Bhuj, Kutch — 3 families

Tie-dye by thumbnail. A single dupatta can carry seventy thousand knots, each tied and dyed by hand across eleven days. No two lengths come out identical, which is the reason the house buys them.

Kadhwa Weaving

Varanasi — 2 weaving families

Each brocade motif is built individually on the loom instead of being floated across the back and cut. Slow, wasteful of time, and the only way to get a saree that looks the same on both faces. Four months per piece.

Pit-Loom Handloom

Chanderi & Maheshwar

Silk-cotton woven on treadle pit looms, which produce a slightly irregular selvedge and a hand no power loom reproduces. The house buys full warps rather than cut lengths, so the weavers are not left with remnants.

Finishing & Cut

Okhla, Delhi — in house

Every garment is cut, canvassed, fitted and hand-finished on site. Nothing is sent out for stitching. Each piece leaves with a card naming the karigars who worked on it and how many hours it took.

Made To Measure

Eight to fourteen weeks, three fittings

01

Consultation

Two hours at the Delhi or Mumbai atelier. We look at silhouette, fabric weight, embroidery density and — most importantly — your calendar. Bring photographs of what you have rejected as well as what you like; it is more useful.

02

Toile

A full calico mock-up is cut to your measurements and fitted before a single metre of silk is touched. Bridal commissions are toiled twice.

03

Embroidery sample

A 30 × 30 cm sample of the exact motif, thread and ground fabric goes to you for approval. Nothing goes onto the adda frame until you have held it.

04

Three fittings

Structure, then balance, then finish. The final fitting is scheduled no later than ten days before the event so there is room to be wrong once.

05

Delivery

Hand-delivered within India, wrapped in unbleached muslin inside a cotton garment bag, with a care card and the names of everyone who worked on it.
